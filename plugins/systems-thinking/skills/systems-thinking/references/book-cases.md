The skill's calibration examples, all from Gharajedaghi, *Systems Thinking*, 3rd ed. (Elsevier, 2011). They're retold in our own words and cite PDF page numbers (the printed page is about the PDF page minus 23). Match the **depth and shape** of each case, not its industry.

## The utility mess (ch 8, pp. 189–199): match this for `formulate-the-mess.md`

**Context.** The setting is a typical regulated utility of the early 1990s ("XYZ"). After the 1979 oil crisis, fear of shortage led to building too much generation while policy pushed demand down. Under fixed-cost regulation, the price per unit rose. Reduced competitiveness, downsizing and layoffs followed, more customers couldn't pay, and pressure to restructure the industry kept growing. The team mapped this whole loop first, as the context for the company (Fig 8.4).

**Themes.** The mess team watched how the system behaved, learned its history, and asked why it acts as it does. Then it grouped the behaviors into seven themes:
1. Success changed the game, and there was no explicit vision
2. A monopolistic, cost-plus regulated environment
3. A non-competitive culture
4. Mediocrity and tolerance of incompetence
5. An input-based personnel policy (pay by job, seniority and headcount, not results)
6. Structural incompatibility and conflict
7. Uncertainty about the future

**The interaction map.** The themes were drawn as one picture of how each reinforces the others (Fig 8.5). For example, the cost-plus environment makes performance criteria irrelevant. Irrelevant criteria plus input-based pay plus the culture together pull everything toward average. The average then needs more layers of hierarchy to get the work done.

**The second-order machine.** Too much of some virtues and neglect of their opposites: cost-plus, input-based, friction-free and stability-seeking, at the expense of performance-based, entrepreneurial and change-oriented. Proven virtues, pushed long enough, became vices. One concrete gear: the wrong criteria were measured accurately (years, headcount, budget size). That rewarded empire-building and pushed people up into jobs they couldn't do.

**The story.** The mess is framed as the legacy of a hugely successful old game, not as anyone's failure. Deregulation changed the game. Insufficient growth would break the built-in cost increases, and competition would turn peaceful coexistence into a zero-sum fight. The story also names the denial pattern: people blame the actors for what the system produces.

**What to copy:**
- themes (seven here), each grounded in observed behavior
- a drawn map, not a list
- one named second-order machine
- the story told as the cost of past success, with no blame

## Butterworth (ch 11, pp. 268–272): match this for `interactive-design.md`

**The mess in brief.** Health care had grown out of sickness care, funded by fee-for-service, cost-plus pricing and third-party payers. Patients asked, providers served, and payers passed the bill on, so nothing disciplined demand. It was a reinforcing loop. Each fix brought a conflict back in a new form:
1. HMOs traded volume for discounts.
2. Then they managed care by bureaucracy, which patients and doctors rejected.
3. Then capitation pushed the risk down onto small groups of doctors, which contradicts how insurance spreads risk.
4. Then special approvals for hard cases put the bureaucrat back in the room.

The pendulum kept swinging.

**The specs.** Each spec answers one named conflict. The new system should:
- **Supply and demand:** give patients the best care without feeding wasteful demand
- **Technology:** use it fully without creating unreasonable expectations
- **Structural conflict:** dissolve it among patients, providers, payers and administrators, so that none of them is compromised
- **Change:** stay flexible and responsive
- **Learning:** keep learning, adapting and renewing
- **Experimentation:** be state of the art without irreversible social experiments
- **The old mess:** contain it and dissolve it, without letting it spill into the new parts

**The design move.** The team did not pick fee-for-service *or* capitation. It folded the virtues of each into a larger architecture, with dimensions for market access, outputs and inputs. The goal was a design that dissolves the old conflict and allows choice without endless swings. They ran six design iterations with the stakeholders to build consensus.

**What to copy:**
- specs written for the whole, each one answering a conflict from the mess
- refusing the either/or
- walling off the old mess from the new parts
- iterating the design with the people inside it

## The telephone company (ch 6.3, pp. 150–152): match this for `check-the-whole.md` and `measure-and-learn.md`

**The situation.** In 1997, internet-era demand swamped a phone company's throughput. Installation, repair, maintenance and capacity expansion all competed for the same overworked technicians. Complaints rose until the regulator imposed heavy penalties.

**What the dynamic model showed:**
1. **The slack was gone.** About a quarter of the lines in each cable were kept spare for failures. Demand for extra lines used them up, so a failed line was switched onto another active line, and that bought only a few days before the next complaint. The fix moved the failure.
2. **A functional structure.** Each activity belonged to a separate department, rewards were for volume, and blame and fights over resources were the norm.
3. **Too many hands in the plant.** More technicians touching the same equipment meant worse records and more failures. Complaints tracked the number of hands.
4. **Rewards for volume.** Overtime earnings were at record highs.

**The redesign.** One modular team per neighborhood owned all the linked work. Its full overtime pay depended on complaints falling below a normal rate, so the reward was for results, not volume. Module managers could limit new installations based on the state of the plant and the spare capacity. Capacity expansion went to the weakest link first, not to wherever was convenient. The mess dissolved within three months.

**What to copy:**
- **For the check:** find where each local fix moves the failure; find the stock that ran out (slack); find who touches the same surface
- **For learning:** move the reward from volume to results, cap intake by spare capacity, and strengthen the weakest link first

## The welfare loop (ch 2, pp. 71–72): match this for `check-the-whole.md` Step 2's trace

**The situation.** A region widens its welfare support so that fewer families are poor. The book uses it to show how an action can end up making more of the very thing it set out to reduce.

**The trace, in our words:**
1. **First effect:** more support costs more, so taxes rise.
2. Higher taxes push wealthier households and firms to leave. The tax base shrinks, revenue falls, and the cost per remaining payer rises. That is a self-feeding loop (R).
3. Generous support draws more people in need into the region. That raises the cost again: a second self-feeding loop.
4. Support can weaken the pull of work, which adds unemployment to the load.
5. Rising cost plus falling revenue: the system heads for collapse.

**What to copy:**
- Start from the first effect and keep following arrows until they return to where they started.
- Expect more than one loop from one action.
- Give each arrow its own delay. A tax change lands in a budget cycle, people moving takes years *(our illustration; the book gives no figures)*.
- Ask "if we stop, does it stop?" Firms that left don't return the day the tax is cut *(our illustration)*: removing the first cause need not remove the effect (p. 71).
